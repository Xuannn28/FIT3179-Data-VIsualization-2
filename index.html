Shifting Shades of Green: Malaysia’s Forest Reserve Over Time (2003–2021)

Explore how forest reserves vary across states and years. Darker greens reflect regions with more preserved forests — a visual reminder of conservation hotspots.

The maps illustrate the spatial distribution of forest reserve coverage across Malaysian states for the years 2003, 2009, 2015, and 2021. Over this period, Johor shows a gradual increase in forest reserve coverage from 2003 to 2015, shifting from the Low to Medium category — likely the result of stronger local reforestation and land management programs. However, by 2021, Johor’s coverage fell notably from 21.8% to 17.6%, hinting at renewed pressures from urban expansion and agricultural land conversion.

Sabah consistently maintains a Very High level of forest reserve coverage across all years, reflecting its large share of permanent forest reserves and long-standing conservation commitments under the Sabah Forestry Department’s sustainable management framework. Sarawak, on the other hand, remains relatively stable within the Medium category, suggesting incremental progress but slower expansion compared to Sabah.

Nationally, Malaysia remains one of the world’s key biodiversity hotspots, with forests covering nearly half of its land area. Yet, varying land-use pressures — including agriculture, urbanization, and infrastructure growth — continue to influence these patterns, especially in Peninsular Malaysia. The observed trends highlight how regional policies and economic priorities shape the pace and extent of forest conservation over time.

When Rain Meets Canopy: Linking Rainfall and Forest Coverage (2003–2021)

Each cell shows how rainfall and forest cover interact across states and years. Dense blue regions suggest a healthy forest-water relationship.

States in Borneo - particularly Sarawak and Sabah - consistently record the highest rainfall levels and largest forest areas, reflecting the dominance of tropical rainforest ecosystems in East Malaysia. In contrast, most Peninsular states exhibit moderate rainfall and smaller forest extents, with limited year-to-year variation. The patterns suggest a weak correlation between rainfall and forest area, implying that forest coverage is more strongly influenced by other factors. According to the Malaysian Meteorological Department, long-term rainfall trends remain relatively stable nationwide, but localized deforestration and urban expansion continue to reshape microclimatic conditions.

Growing Cities, Shrinking Forests: Human Pressure on Nature (2001–2023)

A comparison of Malaysia’s population growth and remaining forest area. How has urbanization reshaped our green landscape?

From 2001 to 2023, total population increased steadily by nearly 50%, reflecting continuous demographic growth. In contrast, forest area experienced a persistent decline, with only a brief recovery between 2001 and 2015 - possibly linked to short-term reforestation or conservation initiatives. This divergence highlights the imbalance between human expansion and environmental regenration. Although the temporary rebound suggests some effectiveness of restoration programs, the overall negative trajectory implies that such efforts were insufficient to offset long-term deforestration pressures.

What Drives the Loss? Human and Natural Causes of Deforestation (2001–2024)

The donut chart breaks down key drivers — from agriculture to wildfires — showing the human footprint behind forest change.

Between 2001 and 2024, forest loss was overwhelming driven by permanent agriculture, accounting for nearly 74% of total deforestration. This reflects the large-scale conversion of forested land into oil palm and rubber plantations, a pattern consistent with Malaysia's agricultural expansion policies over the past two decades. Logging contributed around 20%, suggesting that commercial timber extraction remains a significant, though secondary, driver. Other factors such as infrastructure development, shifting cultivation, and wildfires collectively represented minimal shares. The dominance of agricultural conversion indicates that economic priorities continue to outweigh conservation objectives. Beyond that chart, research by the Global Forest Watch and Malaysian Forestry Department corroborates that sustainable land-use planning and stricter enforcement of agricultural zoning are essential to mitigating further forest degradation.

Lost Trees, Rising Carbon: Deforestation and CO₂ Emissions (2001–2024)

The multi-line chart illustrates how forest loss contributes to carbon emissions. Peaks align with years of intense human and environmental pressure.

Tree cover loss and associated CO₂ emissions display a closely aligned trend, with both peaking sharply in 2014, the year of highest recorded forest loss. While interannual fluctuations are evident, a significant post-2017 decline is observed, reflecting the impact of strengthened conservation policies and shifting land-use practices. Notably, emissions exhibit a steeper drop compared to tree cover loss after 2018, indicating possible improvements in forest management or reductions in biomass density loss per hectare. An unseen insight lies in the decoupling of emissions from deforestration in recent years - suggesting that not all tree loss results in proportional carbon release. This may be due to reforestation effort, slower degradation, or altered logging practices. According to Global Forest Watch and satellite monitoring data, the decline also concides with intensified climate commitments under international frameworks such as the Paris Agreement, which may have influenced policy enforcement in key tropical regions.

Rising Flames: The Role of Fire in Malaysia’s Forest Story

Tracing the Heat: Weekly Fire Alerts (2012–2024)

A closer look at weekly fire activity. Use the overview-detail line chart to explore specific spikes and their long-term patterns.

The weekly fire alert data reveals distinct seasonal and interannual spikes, with the most extreme fire activity occurring between 2013 and 2016 - particularly during the severe 2014 haze episode linked to regioanl dry conditions and land clearing practices. While alerts remain relatively low after 2018, preriodic surges continue, highlighting fire's persistent role in landscape disturbance. The high frequency of alerts in earlier years suggests intense, widespread burning, often associated with slash-and-burn agriculture or peatland fires. However, the number of fire alerts alone does not convey the scale of forest loss they cause. This raises a critical question: how much of this fire activity actually results in forest cover loss?

When Sparks Meet Trees: Fire Alerts and Forest Loss (2001–2024)

This stacked area chart compares the balance between fire frequency and forest conservation, highlighting critical years of disturbance.

While fire alerts peaked dramatically between 2013 and 2016, their direct contribution to tree cover loss in Malaysia remains comparatively limited. This visualization shows that the majority of deforestration stems from other causes, with fire-related loss consistently representing only a small fraction annually. Even in 2016 - when fire alerts spiked and 20.4 thousand hectares were lost to fires - this accounted for just 3.6% of total tree cover loss. The data suggests that although fires are frequent and intense in certain periods, they are not the dominant driver of long-term forest decline. This underscores a critical insight: fire alone does not explain the scale of deforestation observed.
