Shifting Shades of Green: Malaysia’s Forest Reserve Over Time (2003–2021)

Explore how forest reserves vary across states and years. Darker greens reflect regions with more preserved forests — a visual reminder of conservation hotspots.

The maps illustrate the spatial distribution of forest reserve coverage across Malaysian states for the years 2003, 2009, 2015, and 2021. Over this period, Johor shows a gradual increase in forest reserve coverage from 2003 to 2015, shifting from the Low to Medium category — likely the result of stronger local reforestation and land management programs. However, by 2021, Johor’s coverage fell notably from 21.8% to 17.6%, hinting at renewed pressures from urban expansion and agricultural land conversion.

Sabah consistently maintains a Very High level of forest reserve coverage across all years, reflecting its large share of permanent forest reserves and long-standing conservation commitments under the Sabah Forestry Department’s sustainable management framework. Sarawak, on the other hand, remains relatively stable within the Medium category, suggesting incremental progress but slower expansion compared to Sabah.

Nationally, Malaysia remains one of the world’s key biodiversity hotspots, with forests covering nearly half of its land area. Yet, varying land-use pressures — including agriculture, urbanization, and infrastructure growth — continue to influence these patterns, especially in Peninsular Malaysia. The observed trends highlight how regional policies and economic priorities shape the pace and extent of forest conservation over time.

When Rain Meets Canopy: Linking Rainfall and Forest Coverage (2003–2021)

Each cell shows how rainfall and forest cover interact across states and years. Dense blue regions suggest a healthy forest-water relationship.

States in Borneo - particularly Sarawak and Sabah - consistently record the highest rainfall levels and largest forest areas, reflecting the dominance of tropical rainforest ecosystems in East Malaysia. In contrast, most Peninsular states exhibit moderate rainfall and smaller forest extents, with limited year-to-year variation. The patterns suggest a weak correlation between rainfall and forest area, implying that forest coverage is more strongly influenced by other factors. According to the Malaysian Meteorological Department, long-term rainfall trends remain relatively stable nationwide, but localized deforestration and urban expansion continue to reshape microclimatic conditions.
